PAULA — “Can I be happy without leaving him behind?”

Fictional role-play for reviewing conversational process. All biographical details are invented. Oria does not claim contact with the dead or use astrology to prescribe a grief timeline. No birth chart has been calculated.

BACKGROUND

Paula is 58, a librarian whose husband, Martin, died three years ago. She has recently enjoyed spending time with a man from her walking group, who has invited her to dinner. She is surprised by her interest and uneasy about what it means. Her adult daughter reacts awkwardly when Paula mentions him. Paula contacts Oria privately because she wants to speak without managing the feelings of people who knew her husband.

FIRST CONTACT

Paula: I'd like to talk about starting something new when you still love someone who's died. Is that something you discuss?

Oria: Yes. We can make room for both. What has brought the question up now?

Paula: A dinner invitation. I've made it feel like a vote on my entire marriage.

SESSION

Oria: Who invited you?

Paula: His name's Simon. We walk with the same group. He's kind, and he doesn't rush to fill every silence. I said I'd check my diary, which is almost aggressively empty.

Oria: What did you feel when he asked?

Paula: Pleased. Then disloyal. Then annoyed with myself for being disloyal to someone who isn't here to be hurt.

Oria: What does the disloyalty consist of, in your mind?

Paula: Having a story Martin isn't in. Someone learning how I take my tea. Such small things. They feel enormous.

Oria: They sound closely tied to being known by him.

Paula: Yes. People say, “He'd want you to be happy.” I know they're trying to help. But they don't get to put words in his mouth.

Oria: I won't do that either.

Paula: Good. I wondered whether you might. People associate this sort of thing with messages.

Oria: I don't have a message from Martin. What I can do is listen to what your relationship means to you and what you want now.

Paula: I want to have dinner with Simon. There. Apparently that's not difficult to say when nobody looks wounded.

Oria: Who has looked wounded?

Paula: My daughter. I mentioned him and she said, “Already?” It's been three years. Then I felt cruel for counting the years like they qualified me for release.

Oria: How did you answer?

Paula: I said it wasn't a date. I don't actually know if it is. But I said it to make her comfortable.

Oria: What would you have preferred to say?

Paula: “I miss your dad. I'm also lonely. Please don't make me choose which fact you can bear hearing.” Too harsh?

Oria: It carries something important. What do you want her to understand, without making her responsible for settling your loneliness?

Paula: That my love for her father hasn't been deleted. That she doesn't have to be ready to meet anyone. And that I can decide whether to go to dinner.

Oria: Does that sound closer to what you want to communicate?

Paula: Yes. Though I'd probably cry halfway through.

Oria: It can still be a clear conversation if you cry.

[Paula smiles and takes a moment.]

Paula: Where would astrology come into this?

Oria: We could use the image of a cycle to explore what changes and what continues. But I wouldn't tell you a cycle has ended and it's time to move on. Does that imagery help you?

Paula: Not really. It sounds like closing a book. I'm a librarian; I know you can have more than one book open.

Oria: Then let's use your image. What needs to remain open?

Paula: Martin. His stories. Saying his name without apologizing. Our children. The ridiculous holiday we had in Wales.

Oria: And what would you like permission to open?

Paula: An evening where I'm a woman someone wants to know. Not just the surviving half of a couple.

Oria: What would help you go at a pace that feels like yours?

Paula: Asking Simon whether he means dinner as a date. I could say I'm interested and a little out of practice. Then see how he responds.

CLOSING

Oria: What remains uncertain?

Paula: Whether I'll enjoy it. Whether my daughter will be upset. Whether I'll come home and cry. None of those is a reason I can't have dinner.

Oria: What would you like to do?

Paula: Reply. Not promise anyone a new life. Just tell him Thursday works.

PROCESS REVIEW

Surface request: permission to explore romance after bereavement.
Underlying need: continuity with her marriage alongside autonomy and new connection.
Key interaction: Paula rejects imagined messages from her husband and later replaces Oria's metaphor with her own.
Boundary: Oria neither speaks for the deceased nor prescribes a timetable for grief.
Ending: Paula chooses one conversation and one possible date. Grief, family discomfort, and pleasure are allowed to coexist.
